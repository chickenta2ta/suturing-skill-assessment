\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Training surgeons requires assessing their technique, and suturing is one of
the most important skills to assess. An experienced surgeon
watching a trainee can tell a good stitch from a poor one, but such review
takes time that is rarely available, the judgment varies between reviewers,
and ``good'' or ``poor'' alone does not tell the trainee what to change.
EASE~\cite{Haque2022} addresses the last two points by dividing robotic
suturing into sub-skills, each scored against a defined criterion, such as
where along the needle it is held or at what angle, so that a score points to
what to correct. Scoring remains manual, however, and when every stitch is
scored, the rater's time becomes the bottleneck.

Automated skill assessment from surgical video has moved from classifying
expertise on JIGSAWS~\cite{Funke2019,Liu2021} to rating tools with clinically
defined criteria~\cite{Kiyasseh2023}. For EASE, prior
work~\cite{Cui2024,Deo2026} trains networks on annotated stitches to predict
each sub-skill as good or poor, and a general-purpose vision--language model
(VLM) can be asked for a score directly. Either way the score is the output
of a model: attention maps and evidence frames can show where it
looked~\cite{IsmailFawaz2019,Kiyasseh2023b}, but not what it measured, so a
given score cannot be checked. Keypoints and masks of instruments are now
readily obtained from surgical video~\cite{Ghanekar2025}, and the 3D pose of
a suture needle has been recovered for autonomous suturing, but with stereo
cameras, calibrated projection, or trained detectors~\cite{Chiu2022,Li2024}.

Our observation is that EASE already defines its criteria geometrically: the
grasp position along the needle, the angle between the needle and the jaws,
and the depth of the needle in the jaws. We therefore measure these quantities
rather than predict the scores (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). A video contains
many stitches, and each sub-skill refers to a specific part of a stitch, so
events detected in the video first divide it into stitches and then into the
window that each sub-skill refers to. Within the window, keypoints and a
needle mask on each frame give the geometry, which is measured in 3D from the
monocular video and converted into scores by the EASE criteria. Every score
thus comes with the frames and the value it was derived from, and can be
verified.

Our contributions are threefold. (1) A measurement-based formulation of EASE
scoring: a score is a geometric quantity compared with the EASE criterion,
measured within the event-defined window in which the hold does not change,
so that every frame in it is a valid observation; the perception that
provides the keypoints and masks is replaceable, and here needs no training.
(2) Geometric measurement from monocular video without calibration: the grasp
position from a 3D arc model of the needle shared across a video, the hold
angle from segments lifted to 3D, and the depth from a ratio that projection
preserves. (3) Validation on JIGSAWS~\cite{Gao2014} with surgeons split
between development and test: the method reproduces a surgeon's EASE scores
at least as well as direct grading by a state-of-the-art VLM given favorable
conditions, also when the events are detected automatically, and its measured
values follow the EASE ranges, with most errors near a threshold.
